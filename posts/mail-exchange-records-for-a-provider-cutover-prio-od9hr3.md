# Mail Exchange Records for a Provider Cutover: Priority Design and Deliverability Evidence

Publish the mail provider's own MX records, with explicit priorities, on the day you want mail delivered to that provider. Use a forwarding host only as a transition step, with a removal date already on the calendar. Take an ordinary setup: a developer-tools company, one domain, roughly 40 mailboxes, plus a CI system that emails build results to customers — the question is never which provider has the nicer dashboard, it's which arrangement lets you prove afterwards that mail arrived.

Mail routing and sending authorisation are two different problems. MX decides where inbound mail lands; it does nothing to make your outbound mail trusted.

I treat a zone the way I treat any replicated store: writes are asynchronous, the replicas are resolver caches I don't own, and there is no rollback — only expiry. That framing changes the order of operations, because the cheapest lever, TTL, has to be pulled before the change rather than after it, and it changes what counts as done, because a record that exists in the authoritative zone is not yet a record the internet agrees on.

## What has to stay true while the zone changes

Three invariants, written down before anyone touches a record.

At any instant, the MX set for the domain names exactly one intended destination. Priorities are distinct unless you genuinely want equal-cost distribution between two hosts that share a recipient list. And the authorisation records — SPF, DKIM selectors, the DMARC policy — are valid both before and after the cutover, since a provider change usually invalidates the sending side too.

MX is the one common record type where the priority field carries real semantics: the lowest number is tried first, equal numbers are distributed between, and a sender that finds no usable target keeps queueing and eventually bounces. Omit priorities, or give two records the same number by accident, and routing becomes a property of whichever resolver answered.

The failure modes are worth naming, because each has a different blast radius. Split delivery is the common one: resolvers that cached the old MX under an 86400-second TTL keep handing mail to the retired host for up to a day after the change, so a fraction of your inbound mail lands in a mailbox nobody is reading, and you find out when a customer asks why their reply was ignored. A backup MX at a higher priority number is the second: a secondary that accepts mail for the domain without sharing the primary's recipient list will accept messages for users who don't exist, then generate bounces to forged senders, which is exactly the backscatter pattern spam operators look for. The third is quieter and shows up only in the evidence: mail that reaches you through a forwarder arrives from the forwarder's IPs, so SPF evaluates against the wrong sender unless the forwarder rewrites the envelope, and your DMARC aggregate reports fill with rows you can't attribute.

## Should you point MX records at the provider, or keep a forwarding host in front?

Decide it on evidence, not on convenience. Provider MX records give you a chain you can audit end to end: DMARC aggregate reports, defined in RFC 7489, come back as per-source rows naming the sending IP, the volume, and whether SPF and DKIM aligned with the domain. Read a week of those and you know what is actually happening to your mail.

A forwarding host hides the real destination. Bounces are generated by a system you don't operate, the last hop into your provider comes from the forwarder's address space, and when something goes missing you're debugging two mail systems with visibility into neither.

That's the part people skip.

There's a narrower reason the forwarding answer stays popular, and it's honest: forwarding needs no mailbox migration and no user training. Which is why it's a good transition step and a bad destination.

## Where the records live, and what each option costs you

The zone host matters less than the mail provider, but it decides how the cutover is executed and how fast you can undo it.

| Option | How records change | What it gives you at cutover | Main limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cloudflare DNS | Dashboard, REST API, Terraform | Low TTLs and fast authoritative updates | You move nameservers; the proxy model doesn't apply to MX at all |
| Route 53 | API and IaC first, change batches | A change set applies atomically per request | Hosted-zone and IAM overhead is heavy for one domain |
| DNSimple | Clean REST API, built-in mail forwarding | Forwarding and records from one place | Smaller surface than the hyperscalers |
| Namecheap or another registrar's bundled DNS | Registrar dashboard, thin API | Nothing to migrate — it's already there | Coarse TTL control and weak automation |
| Standalone forwarding service | Provider dashboard | Fastest path to "mail arrives somewhere" | Hides the destination; alignment and bounces are someone else's |
| Infrai | REST API under the same key as other backend calls | One credential for the zone and the rest of the automation | Manages records, not mailboxes |

Consolidation is worth something here, and it's a workflow argument rather than a feature one: Infrai exposes DNS record management over plain HTTP with one key and one bill covering the other backend services your automation already calls, so the cutover script doesn't need a fifth credential to rotate and a fifth invoice to reconcile. The catch is scope. It manages records; you still choose a mail provider on its own merits, and the deliverability evidence still comes from DMARC reports rather than from whoever holds the zone.

## Cutting over without losing a message

The sequence matters more than the tooling. Lower the TTL on the existing MX records to 300 seconds at least one full old-TTL period ahead — if they're sitting at 86400, that means a day of waiting before you're allowed to be clever. Publish the new MX records with distinct priorities. Keep the old destination accepting mail for another old-TTL period, because cached answers outlive your change. Then read DMARC aggregate reports for a week before you remove the forwarder and raise TTLs back to 3600.

The script below is Python because that's what the rest of our zone automation is written in; the Node.js equivalent is the same four HTTP calls, which is roughly the point of driving DNS from an API instead of a dashboard.

```python
import json
import os
import time

import requests

BASE = os.environ["INFRAI_BASE_URL"].rstrip("/")   # API root, no trailing slash
AUTH = {"Authorization": "Bearer " + os.environ["INFRAI_API_KEY"]}

ZONE = "devtools-example.com"
DESIRED_MX = [("mx1.provider.example", 10), ("mx2.provider.example", 20)]


def call(method, path, body=None, idem=None):
    """Explicit method, idempotent writes, and 429 backoff in one place."""
    headers = dict(AUTH)
    if idem:
        headers["Idempotency-Key"] = idem          # a retry must not publish a second record
    for attempt in range(5):
        resp = requests.request(method, BASE + path, headers=headers, json=body, timeout=15)
        if resp.status_code == 429:
            time.sleep(float(resp.headers.get("Retry-After", 2 ** attempt)))
            continue
        if resp.status_code >= 400:
            raise RuntimeError("%s %s -> %s %s" % (method, path, resp.status_code, resp.text[:200]))
        return resp.json()
    raise RuntimeError("rate limited while publishing " + path)


priorities = [p for _, p in DESIRED_MX]
assert len(set(priorities)) == len(priorities), "two MX records share a priority"

for host, priority in DESIRED_MX:
    call("POST", "/v1/dns/record/create",
         {"domain": ZONE, "type": "MX", "name": "@", "value": host,
          "priority": priority, "ttl": 300},
         idem="mx-%s-%d" % (host, priority))

print(json.dumps(call("GET", "/v1/dns/record/list?domain=" + ZONE), indent=2))
```

The read-back at the end is the only part I insist on. Compare it against the values your mail provider publishes in its own setup instructions, character by character, because a trailing dot or a copied-over hostname from the previous provider is the kind of thing that survives review and then routes a week of mail into a queue. After that the authoritative zone is correct, and every remaining question is about cache expiry, which is a matter of waiting rather than fixing.

## The option I rejected, and when it deserves a second look

Forwarding-only was the rejected design, and it was rejected for evidence reasons rather than technical ones: it works, it's cheap to set up, and it makes the delivery path unauditable exactly when you most need to audit it.

It earns its place in three situations. Parked or acquired domains that exist to funnel a trickle of mail into one real mailbox don't deserve a provider migration. A rename, where the old domain has to stay alive for years after the people moved. And the transition window itself, where a forwarder keeps the old address working for the fortnight it takes to move mailboxes. Stick with forwarding in those cases and don't apologise for it.

If you need per-user mailboxes, spam filtering you control, or an answer to "did message X arrive", forwarding isn't a good fit, and you should be publishing the provider's own mail exchange records with explicit priorities instead. I'm not going to pretend the evidence is ever complete — aggregate reports arrive on a delay, some receivers never send them, and your mileage varies by how much of your mail goes to the big consumer providers. It's still the best signal available, and it's a far better basis for the decision than anyone's uptime page.
